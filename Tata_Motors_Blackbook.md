## ABSTRACT

This project analyses the consolidated financial statements of **Tata Motors Limited** — one of India's largest automobile manufacturers and the parent of Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) — for five financial years, **2020-21 to 2024-25**. The study uses comparative statements, common-size statements, trend analysis, ratio analysis (13 ratios across liquidity, solvency, profitability and activity) and cash flow analysis. All data are drawn from audited consolidated Ind AS financial statements published by the company.

The analysis shows a decisive financial turnaround. Revenue from operations rose from ₹2,49,795 crore in FY2020-21 to ₹4,39,695 crore in FY2024-25 (a rise of 76.0%); the group moved from a loss of ₹13,395 crore in FY2020-21 to a profit of ₹28,149 crore in FY2024-25; total borrowings fell from ₹1,35,905 crore to ₹62,499 crore; the debt-equity ratio improved from 2.46 times to 0.54 times; and net cash from operating activities remained positive in every year, reaching ₹63,102 crore in FY2024-25. At the same time, the study identifies areas of concern: a current ratio persistently below 1.00, a heavy concentration of earnings in the JLR business, a sharp build-up of intangible assets under development (₹48,182 crore at 31 March 2025) and a reported-profit base that benefited from large deferred-tax credits in FY2023-24.

**Keywords:** Financial statement analysis, ratio analysis, cash flow analysis, Tata Motors, Jaguar Land Rover, automobile industry.

# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Introduction

### 1.1.1 Meaning of Financial Statements
Financial statements are the formal records through which a business reports its financial position and performance to the people who have an interest in it — owners, lenders, employees, regulators and the public. A complete set of financial statements of a company registered in India and following the Indian Accounting Standards (Ind AS) comprises:

1. the **Balance Sheet**, which shows the assets, liabilities and equity of the company at a particular date;
2. the **Statement of Profit and Loss** (including other comprehensive income), which shows the revenue earned and the expenses incurred during a period and, therefore, the profit or loss;
3. the **Statement of Cash Flows**, which shows how cash was generated and used under operating, investing and financing activities;
4. the **Statement of Changes in Equity**; and
5. the **Notes** to the financial statements, including the significant accounting policies.

Listed companies that have subsidiaries also publish **consolidated** financial statements, which present the parent and its subsidiaries as a single economic entity after eliminating inter-company transactions. Tata Motors, whose subsidiaries include the Jaguar Land Rover group, reports in this manner; the consolidated statements are therefore the most complete picture of the business.

### 1.1.2 Meaning of Financial Statement Analysis
Financial statement analysis is the process of examining and interpreting the information contained in financial statements, using systematic tools, in order to understand the past performance, present position and likely future prospects of an enterprise. It does not create new information; it re-arranges and compares the reported figures — across years, across items and across companies — so that patterns, strengths and weaknesses become visible. The raw figure "profit after tax is ₹28,149 crore" becomes meaningful only when it is compared with revenue, with equity, with the previous year and with the cash actually generated; analysis supplies these comparisons.

### 1.1.3 Need for Financial Statement Analysis
Analysis is needed because published statements, by themselves, are voluminous and technical, and a single year's figures say little about direction or quality. In particular, analysis is needed:

- to judge whether the business is **growing, stable or declining**;
- to assess **short-term solvency** — whether current obligations can be met from current resources;
- to assess **long-term solvency** — how much of the business is financed by debt and whether the earnings cover the interest;
- to measure **profitability** and the efficiency with which management uses the resources entrusted to it;
- to separate **recurring** performance from one-off items (for example, exceptional charges or deferred-tax credits), which can distort a single year's profit; and
- to compare the reported profit with the **cash** the business actually generates.

### 1.1.4 Importance of Financial Statement Analysis
Different users rely on analysis for different purposes. **Investors and shareholders** use it to evaluate returns and risk; **lenders and bond-holders** use it to judge repayment capacity; **management** uses it for planning, control and performance evaluation; **employees and trade unions** use it to assess the stability of the employer; **suppliers and dealers** use it to judge creditworthiness; and **regulators and researchers** use it to monitor compliance and study the industry. For a capital-intensive, globally integrated manufacturer such as Tata Motors — which invests heavily in product development and has experienced both severe losses and record profits within five years — disciplined analysis is particularly important to understand what changed and why.

## 1.2 Objectives of Financial Statement Analysis
The objectives of this study, framed around the analysis of Tata Motors, are:

1. To study the structure and changes in the **assets, liabilities and equity** of Tata Motors over five years using comparative and common-size balance sheets.
2. To study the movement in **revenue, expenses and profit** using comparative and common-size statements of profit and loss.
3. To identify the **direction and pace of change** in key items through trend analysis, taking FY2020-21 as the base year.
4. To evaluate the **liquidity, solvency, profitability and activity (efficiency)** of the company through 13 accounting ratios calculated for all five years.
5. To analyse the **cash flow** statement and judge whether operations generate sufficient cash, how much is invested and how the balance is financed.
6. To explain the **reasons** behind major changes, distinguishing operating performance from one-off and accounting effects.
7. To present **findings, suggestions and conclusions** on the overall financial health of the company.

## 1.3 Techniques of Financial Statement Analysis

### 1.3.1 Comparative Statements
Comparative statements place the figures of two or more periods side by side and show the **absolute change** and the **percentage change** in each item. They answer the question "by how much did each item change?"

> Absolute change = Current year figure − Previous year figure
> Percentage change = (Current year − Previous year) ÷ Previous year × 100

### 1.3.2 Common-Size Statements
In a common-size statement every item is expressed as a percentage of a common base — **total assets** (or total liabilities and equity) in the balance sheet and **revenue from operations** in the profit and loss statement. This removes the effect of size and shows the structure of the business and how it changes.

> Common-size percentage = (Item ÷ Base) × 100

### 1.3.3 Trend Analysis
Trend analysis (index-number analysis) expresses each year's figure as a percentage of a **base year** (here FY2020-21 = 100), so that the direction and speed of growth of different items can be compared on one scale.

> Trend index = (Figure of the year ÷ Figure of the base year) × 100

### 1.3.4 Ratio Analysis
A ratio expresses the relationship between two related figures. Ratio analysis converts absolute figures into meaningful relationships and is classified into **liquidity**, **solvency (leverage)**, **profitability** and **activity (turnover)** ratios. The 13 ratios calculated in Chapter 4 are listed in Table 1.1.

### 1.3.5 Cash Flow Analysis
Cash flow analysis studies the statement of cash flows to determine the sources and uses of cash. Profit is an accounting measure and can differ greatly from cash. The analysis therefore examines cash flow from operating, investing and financing activities, the net change in cash, and derived measures such as free cash flow.

## 2.1 Industry Profile

### 2.1.1 Introduction to the Industry
The automobile industry designs, manufactures, markets and services motor vehicles — passenger cars and utility vehicles, commercial vehicles (trucks and buses), two- and three-wheelers, and related components. It is one of the largest manufacturing sectors in most economies because it has long supply chains (steel, aluminium, rubber, electronics, chemicals), employs large numbers of people directly and indirectly, and is closely linked to economic activity: passenger vehicles reflect household income and credit availability, while commercial vehicles reflect freight demand, infrastructure spending and industrial activity.

Tata Motors operates in three of the industry's most important segments — **commercial vehicles** (mainly in India), **passenger vehicles including electric vehicles** (in India), and **premium and luxury vehicles** through Jaguar Land Rover (globally). Its performance therefore depends on both the Indian economy and on luxury-car demand in the UK, Europe, North America and China.

### 2.1.2 Evolution and Development
**Global.** The industry began in the late nineteenth century and was transformed by the moving assembly line, which made cars affordable for the mass market †. Over the twentieth century, production hubs developed in the United States, Europe and Japan, and from the 1990s China and India emerged as major producers and consumers †. In recent years the industry's direction has been reset by three forces: **electrification**, **software-defined and connected vehicles** and **tightening emission regulation**.

**India.** The Indian industry grew slowly under the licensing regime before 1991 †. After economic liberalisation in 1991, foreign manufacturers entered, domestic manufacturers modernised and India became a major production base for small cars, utility vehicles, two-wheelers and commercial vehicles †. Emission norms have steadily tightened (the move to BS-VI norms was completed in 2020 †), which raised the cost and technology content of vehicles and forced manufacturers to invest heavily. The period covered by this study began with the COVID-19 disruption of 2020-21, followed by the global semiconductor shortage that constrained production in FY2021-22 (Tata Motors press release, 12 May 2022).

### 2.1.3 Current Industry Scenario
**India.** According to the Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers (SIAM), as reported in the trade press, the Indian automobile industry recorded **7.3% growth in domestic sales in FY2024-25** and **19.2% growth in exports**. Passenger vehicle sales reached a record of about **4.3 million units** (growth of 2%, on a high base), with **utility vehicles forming 65%** of passenger vehicle sales (up from 60% a year earlier), passenger vehicle exports rose 14.6% to about 0.77 million units, and **electric passenger vehicle sales crossed 100,000 units (+18.2%)**. Commercial vehicles declined slightly by 1.2% in the year (Source: Mobility Outlook, "SIAM Reports Moderate Growth Amidst Changing Dynamics In March 2025").

**Global luxury segment (JLR).** For Tata Motors' largest business, FY2025-26 showed how exposed the luxury segment is to external shocks: JLR's results were affected by a cyber incident that halted production for about five weeks from September 2025, additional US tariffs and China's luxury-vehicle tax (Sources: Just Auto/Yahoo Finance; Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd Q4 FY26 release, 14 May 2026). These events fall after the study period but illustrate the risks described in this chapter.

### 2.1.5 Opportunities
- **Electrification:** growing electric-vehicle penetration in India (more than 100,000 electric passenger vehicles sold in FY2024-25) and global regulation favouring zero-emission vehicles.
- **Domestic demand:** rising incomes, a shift towards utility vehicles, and infrastructure and logistics growth supporting commercial vehicles. Tata Motors reported strong domestic momentum after the GST rate changes of September 2025 ("GST 2.0") (Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd, Q4 FY26 release).
- **Exports and premium demand:** record Indian passenger vehicle exports; strong order books for premium products (JLR reported an order book of more than 168,000 units in May 2022).
- **Technology:** software, connectivity and driver-assistance features that raise revenue per vehicle.

### 2.1.6 Challenges
- **Supply-chain shocks:** the semiconductor shortage limited JLR wholesales to 294,182 units in FY2021-22, down 15% (Tata Motors release, 12 May 2022).
- **Commodity inflation and currency movements:** raw material cost inflation was cited as a key headwind in FY2021-22.
- **High capital intensity:** electric and software platforms require sustained spending; Tata Motors' capex (property, plant and equipment plus intangibles) was ₹38,042 crore in FY2024-25 (S3).
- **Regulation and trade policy:** emission rules, tariffs and luxury taxes.
- **Cyclicality:** vehicle demand falls sharply when economic conditions weaken (COVID-19 in FY2020-21).

### 2.1.7 Future Prospects
The industry outlook is positive but requires heavy investment: electric and hybrid vehicles are expected to take a growing share, connected and software features will become a larger part of vehicle value, and India is expected to remain among the fastest-growing large automobile markets. Companies with strong balance sheets, flexible platforms and focused management will be best placed. Tata Motors' decision to separate its commercial vehicle and passenger vehicle/JLR businesses (Section 2.2.10) is aimed at this kind of focus.

### Interpretation of the Comparative Balance Sheet

**(i) Share capital.** Equity share capital was almost constant at ₹765.81 crore (FY21), ₹765.88 crore, ₹766.02 crore and ₹766.50 crore (FY24); the small increases were due to shares issued when employees exercised stock options (₹0.48 crore in FY24, S2). In FY25 it fell by ₹30.50 crore (−4.0%) to ₹736 crore, because 50.85 crore 'A' Ordinary shares of ₹2 each (₹102 crore) were cancelled and 35.60 crore new Ordinary shares (₹71 crore) were issued to TML Securities Trust under the NCLT-sanctioned scheme that took effect on 1 September 2024 (S3, Note 6). The change is therefore a *restructuring of the share classes*, not a return of capital: there was no fresh equity raised from the market.

**(ii) Reserves (other equity).** Reserves moved with profits, losses and other comprehensive income. They **fell by ₹10,685.55 crore (−19.6%) in FY22** because the group reported a net loss attributable to shareholders of ₹11,441.47 crore (S1). They rose only ₹760.41 crore (+1.7%) in FY23, when the profit of ₹2,414.29 crore was largely offset by losses in other comprehensive income (hedging reserve and cost-of-hedging reserve moved from ₹(5,520) crore and ₹66 crore to ₹(6,556) crore and ₹(1,005) crore; S2 statement of changes in equity). In **FY24 reserves almost doubled (+₹39,595.75 crore; +88.9%)**, driven by the profit attributable to owners of ₹31,399.09 crore, a gain of ₹7,599.45 crore in the hedging reserve, and a gain of ₹3,089.85 crore on the partial (21.3%) sale of Tata Technologies, which is credited directly to retained earnings because control was retained (S2). In FY25 reserves rose a further ₹31,256.48 crore (+37.1%), mainly because of total comprehensive income of ₹34,255 crore attributable to shareholders (S3), partly offset by dividends paid (₹2,303 crore) and expenses and tax on the share-cancellation scheme. **The net effect: reserves, and therefore shareholders' funds, more than doubled over the study period, entirely through retained earnings rather than new share issues.**

**(iii) Borrowings.** Total borrowings rose slightly in FY22 (₹135,905 crore to ₹139,677 crore, +2.8%) — Tata Motors' own release says finance costs rose in FY22 "due to higher gross borrowings" — and then **fell for three consecutive years**: −10.0% in FY23, −21.6% in FY24 and −36.5% in FY25, a total reduction of **₹73,406 crore (−54.0%)** from FY21 to FY25. Non-current borrowings fell 56.8% (₹93,112.77 crore to ₹40,217 crore) and current borrowings fell 47.9% (₹42,791.74 crore to ₹22,282 crore). The reasons are (a) *cash repayments funded by operating cash flow* — in FY24, repayments of long-term borrowings (₹31,675.20 crore) were far larger than new long-term borrowings (₹11,629.40 crore); (b) in FY25, a net cash repayment of about ₹8,059 crore (Table 4.B.4) **plus the derecognition of TMFL's liabilities (₹31,982 crore, which in a finance company would consist largely of borrowings — Data/source verification required in the FY25 borrowings note)** — so the FY25 fall overstates the pure cash de-leveraging. Management's own message at the FY24 results was that the India business was debt-free and that the group was on track to become net automotive-debt free in FY25.

**(iv) Fixed assets.** Fixed assets fell 6.6% in FY22 and 1.8% in FY23, because annual depreciation and amortisation (₹24,836 crore in FY22) exceeded capital spending (₹15,168 crore of cash capex in FY22) in a period when production was constrained. They then rose 7.3% in FY24 and **15.6% in FY25 to ₹181,503 crore**. The composition changed sharply: property, plant and equipment declined 8.9% (₹79,640 crore to ₹72,536 crore) and other intangible assets (capitalised product development) fell 34.7% (₹51,773 crore to ₹33,790 crore) as they were amortised, while **intangible assets under development rose 282.8% (₹12,587 crore to ₹48,182 crore)** and capital work-in-progress rose 110.4% (₹8,377 crore to ₹17,624 crore). IAUD now represents 26.5% of fixed assets. This is the accounting footprint of JLR's electrification programme: the FY24 auditor's report identifies capitalisation of JLR product engineering costs, including those for the transition to battery electric vehicles, as a key audit matter. These projects are not yet amortised, so they have not yet reduced profit — but they are also a source of risk if launches are delayed.

**(v) Investments.** Total investments (non-current plus current) rose from ₹24,620 crore to ₹35,656 crore (+44.8%). The main movement is in **current investments**, which dropped to ₹14,253 crore in FY24 and then jumped 90.8% to ₹27,199 crore in FY25, because surplus cash was placed in mutual funds (net investment of ₹12,603 crore in FY25, S3 cash flow). Non-current investments (mainly equity-accounted joint ventures and associates such as the Chery JLR joint venture) rose steadily from ₹5,569 crore to ₹8,457 crore.

**(vi) Current assets.** Current assets were virtually unchanged in FY22 (−0.1%), rose 2.9% in FY23 and 11.3% in FY24 (inventories +17.3% and cash and bank +23.7%, as sales volumes and prices rose), and fell 4.7% in FY25. The FY25 decline is explained by the **derecognition of current finance receivables (₹24,070 crore at 31 March 2024 to nil)**, partly offset by higher current investments (+₹12,946 crore) and other current financial assets (+₹10,863 crore).

**(vii) Current liabilities.** Current liabilities followed the same pattern: −4.5% (FY22), +2.9%, +12.0% and −4.0% (FY25). Trade payables and acceptances — suppliers' credit — grew from ₹76,040 crore to ₹97,368 crore (+28.0%) as purchases grew with volumes, while current borrowings fell from ₹42,792 crore to ₹22,282 crore. In other words, **operations are increasingly financed by supplier credit rather than by short-term bank borrowing**.

**(viii) Total assets.** Total assets were ₹343,126 crore in FY21, fell 3.6% in FY22, rose 1.7% in FY23, 10.3% in FY24 and 2.2% in FY25 to ₹378,642 crore (+10.4% over four years). The FY25 increase understates underlying growth because TMFL's assets of ₹37,627 crore were derecognised: adding them back, FY25 assets would have been about ₹416,269 crore, or roughly 12.3% above FY24 *(indicative calculation: (378,642 + 37,627) ÷ 370,664 − 1)*. The mix of assets thus shifted from financial assets and cash towards fixed assets and intangibles under development.

**Overall.** The balance sheet has been **re-shaped in four years**: shareholders' funds have risen strongly, borrowings have been halved, and the asset base has moved towards long-term product investments.

### Interpretation of the Comparative Statement of Profit and Loss

**(i) Revenue.** Revenue from operations grew from ₹249,795 crore (FY21) to ₹439,695 crore (FY25), an increase of 76.0% and a compound annual growth rate of **15.2%** *(workings: (439,695 ÷ 249,794.75)^(1/4) − 1 = 1.7602^0.25 − 1 = 15.2%)*. Growth was +11.5% in FY22 (recovery from COVID-19, with Tata CV revenue up 58% and Tata PV revenue up 90%, but JLR wholesales down 15% to 294,182 units because of the semiconductor shortage; S1 release), **+24.2% in FY23 and +26.6% in FY24**, when JLR volumes, prices and mix improved (JLR's FY24 revenue of £29.0 billion was 27% above the previous year; Tata Motors Q4 FY24 release). FY25 growth was only **+0.4% as reported and +1.3% like-for-like** (Table 3.2(e)), because the Indian commercial and passenger vehicle segments declined (−4.7% and −7.5%) and JLR's revenue was broadly flat.

**(ii) Operating expenses.** Operating expenses rose 16.7%, 23.8%, 20.5% and 1.4% in the four years, broadly in line with revenue. The largest component, the cost of goods sold, grew from ₹158,292 crore to ₹271,786 crore (+71.7%) — slightly slower than revenue (+76.0%) — so material cost fell as a share of revenue (Table 3.4). **Employee cost** rose 72.8% (₹27,648 crore to ₹47,767 crore); **product development/engineering expense** doubled from ₹5,227 crore to ₹10,716 crore (+105.0%), mostly in FY22 (+76.2%); and other expenses net of capitalisation rose from ₹28,073 crore to ₹54,294 crore. The amount transferred to capital and other accounts — essentially development and construction cost that is capitalised rather than expensed — grew from ₹12,849 crore to ₹31,105 crore; this reduces reported expenses and raises capital spending, which is why it matters for the cash flow analysis in Chapter 4.

**(iii) Finance costs.** Finance costs rose from ₹8,097 crore to ₹9,312 crore (+15.0%) in FY22 because of higher gross borrowings (S1 release), and ₹10,225 crore in FY23, and then fell to ₹9,986 crore (FY24) and **₹5,083 crore in FY25 (−49.1%)**. The FY25 drop reflects (a) the 36.5% reduction in total borrowings in FY25 and the 21.6% reduction in FY24 (Table 3.1), and (b) the exclusion of TMFL's interest cost, which is now in discontinued operations (Table 3.2(e): from ₹7,594 crore re-presented FY24 to ₹5,083 crore).

**(iv) Depreciation and amortisation.** Depreciation was steady at ₹23,547 crore to ₹24,860 crore in FY21–FY23, rose to ₹27,270 crore in FY24 (+9.7%), and then fell 14.7% to ₹23,256 crore in FY25 even though fixed assets rose 15.6%. This is consistent with a large part of the new investment sitting in intangible assets under development (not yet amortised) and with older capitalised development costs becoming fully amortised. *Data/source verification required:* the specific reason for the FY25 reduction should be checked in the notes on property, plant and equipment and intangible assets of the FY25 annual report.

**(v) Profit before tax.** The group moved from a **loss before tax of ₹10,853 crore in FY21** to a loss of ₹7,077 crore in FY22, **profit of ₹3,394 crore in FY23, ₹27,955 crore in FY24 and ₹33,780 crore in FY25**. FY21 was depressed by an *exceptional charge of ₹13,761 crore*, of which ₹14,994.30 crore related to JLR restructuring (asset write-downs and employee and third-party obligations) (S1); excluding exceptional items, FY21 would have shown a profit of ₹2,908 crore *(workings: −10,853.24 + 13,761.02)*. FY22 was hit by the chip shortage, commodity inflation and a ₹428.66 crore provision for the Russia market. The recovery from FY23 reflects higher volumes and prices at JLR, a better mix (Range Rover and Defender), lower finance costs and cost-reduction programmes. FY24 was an all-time-high year at the operating level (EBITDA of ₹62.8 thousand crore according to the company's own definition, Q4 FY24 release).

**(vi) Tax.** The tax line is the most volatile item. A tax **credit of ₹3,852 crore in FY24** (effective rate −13.8%) arose mainly from the recognition of **deferred tax assets** on previously unrecognised tax losses — the auditors' key audit matters for FY24 cite recognition of JLR's UK tax losses and ₹1,248.90 crore of deferred tax asset on brought-forward business losses of the holding company (S2). In FY25 the group recorded a tax **expense of ₹10,502 crore (effective rate 31.1%)**, including a deferred-tax charge of ₹5,479 crore, as these assets began to be used or were re-assessed. *Data/source verification required:* the precise explanation for the FY25 deferred-tax charge should be taken from Note 22 of the FY25 annual report. **Consequently, FY24 profit after tax (₹31,807 crore) is not a clean measure of recurring earnings; profit before tax is a better guide.**

**(vii) Profit after tax.** Profit for the year improved from a **loss of ₹13,395 crore (FY21)** to a loss of ₹11,309 crore (FY22), then profits of ₹2,690 crore (FY23), ₹31,807 crore (FY24) and ₹28,149 crore (FY25). The FY25 profit is not directly comparable with FY24: it includes a one-time gain of ₹4,871 crore from discontinued operations (mainly the ₹4,975 crore gain on the TMFL merger), whereas profit from continuing operations was ₹23,278 crore, which is **28.3% lower than the re-presented FY24 figure of ₹32,453 crore** — a decline caused almost entirely by the swing in tax (a ₹10,502 crore charge against a ₹4,024 crore credit), because *profit before tax rose 18.8%* on a like-for-like basis.

**Overall.** The operating recovery is genuine and large — profit before tax improved by ₹44,633 crore between FY21 and FY25 *(workings: 33,780 − (−10,853) = 44,633)* — but the reported after-tax profits of FY24 and FY25 are influenced by deferred tax and by the TMFL gain, so profit before tax and operating cash flow are the more reliable indicators.

### Interpretation of the Common-Size Balance Sheet

**Conclusion.** The common-size view confirms that the balance sheet is now more equity-funded, less dependent on borrowings, and more heavily invested in long-term product assets; but the dependence on supplier credit has increased.

### Interpretation of the Trend Analysis

# CHAPTER 5: FINDINGS, SUGGESTIONS AND CONCLUSION

## 5.1 Major Findings

All findings below are based on the analysis in Chapters 3 and 4. Figures are consolidated, in ₹ crore, for FY2020-21 to FY2024-25 unless stated otherwise.

1. **Revenue grew by 76.0% in four years, but momentum stopped in FY25.** Revenue from operations rose from ₹249,795 crore to ₹439,695 crore (compound growth of 15.2% a year). Growth was 11.5%, 24.2% and 26.6% in FY22-FY24 but only 0.4% in FY25 (1.3% on a like-for-like basis excluding the finance subsidiary).
2. **The group moved from heavy loss to substantial profit.** Profit before tax rose from a loss of ₹10,853 crore (FY21) to a profit of ₹33,780 crore (FY25), and profit for the year from a loss of ₹13,395 crore to a profit of ₹28,149 crore; the net profit margin moved from −5.36% to 6.40%.
3. **The operating margin is no higher than in FY21; the profit improvement is largely due to lower capital charges and the end of exceptional losses.** The operating profit ratio was 12.93% in FY21 and 12.77% in FY25, while depreciation fell from 9.43% to 5.29% of revenue, finance cost from 3.24% to 1.16%, and exceptional items from 5.51% to 0.13%. The gross profit ratio improved from 36.63% to 38.19%.
4. **The quality of reported profit varies by year.** FY24 profit (₹31,807 crore) included a tax *credit* of ₹3,852 crore, mostly due to recognition of deferred tax assets; FY25 profit (₹28,149 crore) includes ₹4,871 crore from discontinued operations. Profit from continuing operations in FY25 (₹23,278 crore) was 28.3% below the re-presented FY24 figure, although profit before tax rose 18.8%.
5. **Short-term liquidity is structurally tight but stable.** The current ratio was 0.93-0.97 and the quick ratio 0.68-0.74 in all five years; current liabilities exceeded current assets by ₹6,873 crore at 31 March 2025. The business runs on supplier credit (trade payables and acceptances = 25.7% of total assets), but it holds ₹68,033 crore of cash, bank balances and current investments.
6. **Solvency improved dramatically.** Total borrowings fell by ₹73,406 crore (−54.0%), the debt-equity ratio from 2.46 (peak 3.13 in FY22) to 0.54, the proprietary ratio rose from 16.1% to 30.7%, and interest cover from −0.34 to 7.65 times. Part of the FY25 reduction is due to the derecognition of the finance subsidiary's liabilities (₹31,982 crore).
7. **Shareholders' equity more than doubled — through retained earnings, not new capital.** Equity attributable to owners rose from ₹55,247 crore to ₹116,144 crore (+110.2%); share capital was unchanged apart from a restructuring of share classes in FY25.
8. **Returns on capital recovered strongly.** ROCE moved from −1.49% to 18.34% and ROE from −24.35% to 23.96% (peak 36.98% in FY24, inflated by the tax credit).
9. **Asset efficiency improved on every measure.** Total asset turnover rose from 0.73 to 1.16 times; inventory turnover from 4.39 to 5.75 times (holding period from 83 to 63 days); debtors turnover from 19.7 to 33.2 times (collection period from 18.5 to 11.0 days).
10. **The asset base is shifting towards product development.** Intangible assets under development rose from ₹12,587 crore to ₹48,182 crore (3.67% to 12.72% of total assets) and capital work-in-progress from ₹8,377 crore to ₹17,624 crore; total assets rose only 10.4%.
11. **Operating cash flow was positive in every year and is now very strong.** It rose from ₹29,001 crore (FY21) to ₹67,915 crore (FY24) and ₹63,102 crore (FY25), and has exceeded operating profit (conversion ratio 1.11-1.14) since FY23.
12. **Capital expenditure is rising rapidly.** Capex was ₹38,042 crore in FY25 (8.7% of revenue; 2.5 times the FY22 level), reducing free cash flow from ₹36,502 crore in FY24 to ₹25,060 crore in FY25 (−31.3%).
13. **The group has used its cash to repay lenders first.** Net repayments of borrowings were ₹18,977 crore, ₹28,585 crore and ₹8,059 crore in FY23-FY25; dividends resumed in FY24 (₹769 crore) and rose to ₹2,303 crore in FY25 (8.3% of profit attributable to owners).
14. **The group's results depend on one business.** JLR contributes 71.1% of segment revenue and most of the segment profit (FY25), while the Indian passenger vehicle segment earned a margin of only 0.97% and revenue in both Indian segments fell in FY25.
15. **Comparability caveat.** FY25 figures exclude the finance subsidiary (merged with Tata Capital) — finance receivables of ₹30,164 crore at 31 March 2024 are nil at 31 March 2025 (only ₹171 crore of loans remain) — so some FY25 improvements (lower debt, lower interest, lower depreciation, higher asset turnover) are partly structural rather than operational.

## 5.2 Financial Strengths

1. **Large and diversified revenue base** — ₹439,695 crore across commercial vehicles, passenger/electric vehicles and luxury vehicles.
2. **Very strong operating cash flow** — ₹63,102 crore in FY25, positive in every one of the five years, and 1.66 times capex in FY25.
3. **Much stronger capital structure** — debt-equity 0.54, proprietary ratio 30.7%, borrowings halved in four years.
4. **Comfortable interest cover** — 7.65 times in FY25 against negative cover in FY21.
5. **High returns** — ROCE of 18.3% and ROE of 24.0% in FY25.
6. **Improved asset efficiency** — asset turnover of 1.16 times, inventory holding period of about 63 days and receivables collected in about 11 days.
7. **Strong liquid resources** — ₹68,033 crore of cash, bank balances and current investments (3.05 times current borrowings).
8. **Backing and governance of the Tata Group** — Tata Sons held 46.29% of ordinary shares at 31 March 2024, with an unmodified audit opinion for FY25.

## 5.3 Financial Weaknesses

1. **Current ratio below 1.00 in all years** and quick ratio of about 0.7: reliance on supplier credit.
2. **Concentration of earnings in JLR** (71% of revenue), exposing the group to sterling, US tariff, China demand and cyber risks (FY2025-26 events).
3. **Operating margin has not improved since FY21** (12.77% against 12.93%); employee and other expenses grew faster than revenue in FY25.
4. **Reported profit quality** — FY24 tax credit (₹3,852 crore) and FY25 discontinued-operation gain (₹4,871 crore); FY25 tax charge of ₹10,502 crore.
5. **Rising capitalisation of development cost** — intangible assets under development of ₹48,182 crore (12.72% of total assets) and ₹31,105 crore of costs transferred to capital in FY25 (7.07% of revenue); profit is flattered until these assets are amortised.
6. **Stagnant revenue in FY25** (+0.4%) and a drop of 31.3% in free cash flow.
7. **Weak profitability of the Indian passenger vehicle business** (segment margin 0.97% in FY25).
8. **Data comparability limits** because of the TMFL deconsolidation and the foreign-currency translation of JLR.

## 5.4 Suggestions and Recommendations

1. **Strengthen working-capital liquidity.** Retain a liquidity buffer of cash, current investments and committed credit lines at least equal to about three times current borrowings (the FY25 level) and gradually raise the current ratio towards 1.0 by extending funding maturities rather than depending on payables; monitor supplier-credit concentration, since a production shock can quickly reduce liquidity.
2. **Raise the operating margin, not only the net margin.** Because FY25's operating margin (12.77%) is no better than FY21's (12.93%), management should target employee and other expense growth below revenue growth (in FY25 they grew 12.4% and 8.3% against revenue growth of 0.4%) and continue the cost and efficiency programmes at JLR (the company has indicated savings targets of £1.7 billion under its 'Enterprise Missions' — TMPVL Q4 FY26 release).
3. **Apply stage-gate discipline to capitalised development spend.** With ₹48,182 crore in intangibles under development, the company should review project viability regularly, disclose the capitalisation rate and expected amortisation start dates, and ensure that launches do not slip, so that impairment risk is limited.
4. **Reduce earnings concentration.** Lift Indian passenger-vehicle margin from about 1% by scaling EV and ICE volumes and cost, build on the commercial-vehicle margin (9.08%), and reduce currency exposure to sterling through hedging and by growing non-JLR earnings; the demerger should be used to give each business its own targets.
5. **Improve earnings-quality reporting.** Management and analysts should highlight profit before tax and exceptional items, operating cash flow, and a "normalised" tax rate, so that deferred-tax credits and one-time gains do not distort performance assessment.
6. **Adopt a clear, rule-based dividend and capital-allocation policy.** FY25 dividend payments (₹2,303 crore) were only 8.3% of profit and 9.2% of free cash flow (computed: 2,303 ÷ 25,060); a stated payout range linked to free cash flow after capex would reward shareholders while protecting the balance-sheet.
7. **Maintain the low-debt position.** Keep debt-equity below about 1.0 and interest cover above about 5 times, and use any surplus first for high-return investment and then for dividends; avoid re-leveraging to fund capex when operating cash flow is sufficient.
8. **Build resilience against external shocks.** Strengthen cyber-security, diversify supplier and chip sources and manage tariff and regulatory risk by regional production flexibility, as the semiconductor shortage in FY22 and the cyber incident of September 2025 showed how quickly production and cash flow can be disrupted.

## 5.6 Conclusion

This study analysed the consolidated financial statements of Tata Motors Limited for the five years from FY2020-21 to FY2024-25 using comparative statements, common-size statements, trend analysis, 13 ratios and cash flow analysis. The conclusions, dimension by dimension, are as follows.

- **Liquidity.** The company's liquidity ratios are below conventional benchmarks (current ratio 0.93-0.97; quick ratio 0.68-0.74) because the business is financed largely by supplier credit and has fast-turning receivables and inventory. The ratios have been stable and are backed by ₹68,033 crore of cash and investments, so liquidity is judged **adequate**, though it is sensitive to production disruptions.
- **Solvency.** Solvency has improved dramatically: borrowings were reduced by 54%, debt-equity fell from 2.46 to 0.54, the proprietary ratio rose from 16% to 31% and interest cover from negative to 7.65 times. Solvency is now **strong**, although part of the FY25 improvement reflects the deconsolidation of the finance subsidiary.
- **Profitability.** The group moved from a net loss of 5.36% of revenue to a net profit of 6.40%, with ROCE of 18.3% and ROE of 24.0%. Profitability is **strong**, but the operating margin has merely returned to its FY21 level, and reported profits in FY24 and FY25 were influenced by deferred tax and a one-off gain.
- **Efficiency.** Asset, inventory and debtor turnover all improved, reflecting 76% higher revenue on only 10% more assets. Efficiency is **strong**, but it will be tested as heavy investment in intangibles enlarges the asset base.
- **Cash flow.** Operating cash flow was positive in every year and reached ₹63,102-67,915 crore in FY24-FY25; it funded record capex (₹38,042 crore) and the repayment of borrowings and still left a positive free cash flow. Cash flow is the **strongest** element of the financial profile.
- **Overall financial health.** Tata Motors is **financially healthy and much stronger than at the start of the study period**. Its principal risks are concentration on JLR, the large unfinished development pipeline and the thin margin of the Indian passenger vehicle business. The demerger effective 1 October 2025 should allow each business to be managed and financed on its own merits. If JLR's margins and the Indian businesses' growth are sustained, the company is well positioned to continue to invest from internally generated cash.

*A final note on FY2025-26: after the study period, JLR was affected by a cyber incident, US tariffs and China's luxury-vehicle tax, and the restructured Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles group reported consolidated revenue of ₹335,582 crore (−8.3%) for FY2025-26 (company release, 14 May 2026). These figures are on a different (post-demerger) basis and do not alter the analysis of the five years above, but they illustrate the external risks identified in the SWOT analysis.*

### B.2 Re-arrangements made for comparability (no source figure was altered)
1. **Share of profit/(loss) of joint ventures and associates.** In S1 (FY21, FY22) it is shown *after* tax (₹(378.96) crore and ₹(74.06) crore); in S2 and S3 it is shown *before* tax. For the five-year series it is treated as part of profit before tax in all years. Effect: FY21 PBT = −10,474.28 − 378.96 = −10,853.24; FY22 PBT = −7,003.41 − 74.06 = −7,077.47. Profit for the year is unchanged (FY21: −10,853.24 − 2,541.86 = −13,395.10; FY22: −7,077.47 − 4,231.29 = −11,308.76).
2. **Held-for-sale assets** are shown outside current assets in all years (in S1 they are included in the current-asset subtotal; they are deducted: FY21 146,887.64 − 220.80 = 146,666.84; FY22 146,977.54 − 523.85 = 146,453.69).
3. **FY22 and FY21 cost of materials** include the line "Basis adjustment on hedge-accounted derivatives" (FY22: 159,598.06 + 1,322.50 = 160,920.56; FY21: 141,392.43 − 35.16 = 141,357.27).
4. **Exceptional items** are totalled from the individual lines in the published statement (workings in Annexure A.2).

### B.3 Formula sheet
| Ratio / measure | Formula |
|:--|:--|
| Absolute change | Current year − Previous year |
| % change | (Current − Previous) ÷ absolute value of Previous × 100 |
| Common-size % | Item ÷ Base (total assets or revenue) × 100 |
| Trend index | Year figure ÷ Base-year (FY2020-21) figure × 100 |
| CAGR | (Closing ÷ Opening)^(1/n) − 1; n = 4 |
| Current ratio | Current assets ÷ Current liabilities |
| Quick ratio | (Current assets − Inventories) ÷ Current liabilities |
| Debt-equity ratio | Total borrowings ÷ Equity attributable to owners |
| Proprietary ratio | Equity attributable to owners ÷ Total assets |
| Interest coverage ratio | EBIT ÷ Finance costs (EBIT = PBT + Finance costs) |
| Gross profit ratio | (Revenue from operations − COGS) ÷ Revenue from operations × 100 |
| Operating profit ratio | Operating profit ÷ Revenue from operations × 100 |
| Net profit ratio | Profit for the year ÷ Revenue from operations × 100 |
| ROCE | EBIT ÷ (Total assets − Current liabilities) × 100 |
| ROE | Profit attributable to owners ÷ Equity attributable to owners × 100 |
| Inventory turnover | COGS ÷ Closing inventory |
| Debtors turnover | Revenue from operations ÷ Closing trade receivables |
| Total asset turnover | Revenue from operations ÷ Closing total assets |
| Free cash flow | Net cash from operating activities − (Payments for PPE + Payments for intangibles) |
| Cash conversion | Net cash from operating activities ÷ Operating profit |
